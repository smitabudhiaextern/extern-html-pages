What you'll see
All three scenario columns are labeled "FY2027". There are no Bull/Base/Bear projections for 2028, 2029, or 2030. Scenario analysis only touches one year.
Why only one year?
The stock will react to FY2027 in the next couple of earnings cycles — that's where the bet lives. Years 2028 and beyond are too far out for scenarios to be useful. They're better left to the DCF, which uses one forecast.
Common confusion
Don't confuse this with the 5-year forecast in BL through BO. That's one story projected 5 years. This is three stories projected 1 year. Different jobs.

Bull · Driver 1
Click cell
BR117
Recurring revenue growth · Bull
What you'll see
Value: 4.0%. Base is 3.0%. Notice the text color is blue — that's the Excel convention for manual analyst input (as opposed to a formula).
How did the analyst pick 4%?
Not arbitrary. A good Bull case assumption can be defended at least one of these ways:
  • Recent best year — what's the company's highest growth rate in the last 3–5 years?
  • Top analyst estimate — the highest growth number on the Street.
  • Management's high-end guidance — the top of their own forecast range.
  • What your thesis implies — e.g., "AI client wins should add ~100 bps to recurring growth" → 3% + 1% = 4%.

For Alight, +100 bps over Base is a defensible bump — meaningful, but not fantasy.
Chain reaction
When this 4.0% changes, it flows through to BR4 (Revenue) → BR7 (Gross Profit) → BR11 (Operating Income) → BR28 (EPS). One cell, the whole income statement re-runs.

How did the analyst pick 38%?
A defensible Bull margin can be anchored to:

Bull · Driver 4

BR46
SG&A Margin · Bull
What you'll see
Value: 19%. Identical to Base. The analyst didn't flex SG&A margin in the Bull case.
How did the analyst decide not to flex SG&A?
Two reasons to leave it alone:
  • Fixed-cost nature — SG&A is mostly salaries, rent, and software. These don't grow or shrink proportionally with revenue in one year.
  • No bull-thesis driver — nothing in the "new client wins" story implies overhead changes. So flexing it would be unjustified.

The discipline: if your story doesn't move the input, leave it locked. Don't manufacture changes just to make Bull look different from Base.
The lesson
Just because an input can be flexed doesn't mean it should be. If the story doesn't move it, leave it alone.

Step 12 of 22
Capital allocation
Click cell
BR40
Share Buyback Price Assumption · Bull
What you'll see
Value: $12. Higher than Base ($10) and much higher than Bear ($5).
How did the analyst pick $12?
The buyback price should be internally consistent with the scenario's price target. The logic:
  • If Bull EPS comes out to $0.14 and the stock trades at, say, ~100× P/E, the Bull price target is ~$14.
  • During the year, the stock averages somewhere between today's $7 and the Bull target of $14 — call it ~$12.
  • That's the price management would pay on average when buying back stock.

Same logic for Bear ($5) and Base ($10).
Subtle but important
Bull and Bear should be logically consistent — if the operating performance is bullish, the stock price assumption should be bullish too. This is a sanity check on your work.

Shares Repurchased · Bull
What you'll see
Value: −12.5M shares. Bigger buyback than Base (−5M).
How did the analyst pick −12.5M?
Buyback size in scenarios is grounded in:
  • Management's authorization — has the board approved a repurchase program? How much capacity is left?
  • Cash flow capacity — in Bull, more cash gets generated, so management can afford a bigger buyback.
  • Prior years — what's the largest buyback the company has done recently?

Bull = 2.5× the Base buyback. Defensible if management has flagged that more cash means more capital return.
The EPS lever
Capital allocation is the fourth driver because share count divides into net income to give you EPS. Fewer shares = higher EPS, all else equal.

Recurring revenue growth · Bear
What you'll see
Value: −4.0%. Compare to BR117: +4.0%. The swing from Bull to Bear is 8 full percentage points.
How did the analyst pick −4%?
Bear-case growth should reflect a specific bad outcome, not just "things go badly":
  • Recent worst year — what's the worst growth rate the company has posted in the last 5–10 years?
  • Recession playbook — what did revenue do in 2008, 2020, etc.?
  • Client concentration math — what would losing the top 1–2 customers do to growth?
  • Sector pressure — peers losing share to a new entrant or technology shift.

For Alight, −4% represents a recession + 1–2 large client losses. Painful but plausible.
Asymmetric tail risk

Step 15 of 22
Bear · Compare
Click cell
BS45
Gross Margin · Bear
What you'll see
Value: 30%. Compare to BR45's 38% — that's an 8-point compression in Bear.
How did the analyst pick 30%?
Bear-case margin should reflect operational leverage in reverse:
  • Historical trough — what's the lowest gross margin the company hit in past downturns?
  • Fixed-cost math — if revenue falls 4% and ~30% of COGS is fixed, gross margin compresses several points just from deleveraging.
  • Cost-cutting lag — management can't shrink headcount overnight, so margins absorb the hit for at least a year.

For Alight, dropping from 37% to 30% (a 7-point hit) is consistent with what could happen if recurring revenue falls and the cost base stays sticky.
What this tells you
Margin compression is what makes Bear scenarios really bad. Revenue declining is one thing — margin contracting at the same time is what tips a stock from "down 20%" to "down 50%".

Step 16 of 22
Bear · Compare
Click cell
BS46
SG&A Margin · Bear
What you'll see
Value: 21%. Compare to BR46 (19%, identical to Base). SG&A margin only flexes in Bear.
How did the analyst pick 21%?
Two anchors for the math:
  • Fixed-cost share — if ~80% of SG&A is fixed (salaries, rent, software), those costs stay constant in absolute terms even if revenue falls 4%.
  • The ratio math — same fixed cost ÷ smaller revenue = higher SG&A margin. A 4% revenue decline naturally pushes SG&A margin up ~1–2 points without any management mistakes.

+2 points (19% → 21%) is consistent with deleveraging on a 4% revenue drop. No assumed extra spending — just the math of fixed costs on a smaller base.
The asymmetry
This is the cost of operating leverage. The same machinery that drops to the bottom line when revenue grows takes a bigger bite when revenue falls. Bear cases are always worse than the symmetric mirror of Bull.
