Project 4 · Step 3 · The Bots Problem

What Bots Look Like
in Checkout Data

Bots leave fingerprints. Six of them show up again and again in checkout data. For each one, you'll see what it looks like, why it points to automation, and what it actually found in Breaking Games' data.

Signal 1 of 6

A lot of one product in a single cart

One cart holding 6, 8, or 12 copies of the same game. Look at the lineitem_quantity column.

Why it points to automation

Most people buy one copy of a board game, maybe two as gifts. A reseller might buy more, but resellers usually finish the purchase. A big quantity sitting in an abandoned cart is worth a look.

Found something. 30 rows across 28 carts have a quantity above 5. The largest is 12 copies of GAME of PHONES, all in one cart. This is the one signal that actually fires here.

Signal 2 of 6

The same email starting many carts

One email address tied to six, ten, twenty separate carts, with nothing ever purchased.

Why it points to automation

A real shopper might restart checkout once or twice if something goes wrong. They don't do it twenty times. A script in a loop does exactly that.

Ruled out. No email in this data has more than 3 carts. Nothing here looks like a loop, so you can cross this off and say so.

Signal 3 of 6

Activity clustered in the dead of night

A pile of carts created between roughly 3 and 5am, or spread evenly across all 24 hours with no quiet stretch at all.

Why it points to automation

People sleep, so real shopping has a rhythm: busy in the day, quiet overnight. Scripts run on a timer and don't care what time it is, so their traffic looks flat or oddly concentrated.

Ruled out. The quietest hours are 1 and 2am (18 and 15 carts) and the busiest are noon and 1pm (78 each). That rise-and-fall is what human shopping looks like. No overnight cluster.

Signal 4 of 6

One email, two different countries

The same email address attached to carts with different billing_country values.

Why it points to automation

A person's billing country doesn't change between two checkouts on the same afternoon. When it does, you're usually looking at test data or stolen card details being tried out.

Ruled out. No email in this data spans more than one billing country. Rare signal, but high confidence when it does appear, so it's worth keeping on your checklist.

Signal 5 of 6

A cart worth $0

A checkout that exists but has a subtotal of zero, as if nothing was ever added to it.

Why it points to automation

Real customers don't reach checkout with an empty cart. A $0 cart usually means something created it programmatically rather than a person clicking "add to cart."

Ruled out, with a warning. Zero carts here have a $0 subtotal. But 305 rows have a blank subtotal, and those are not bots: in a multi-item cart, only the first row carries the cart total. Treat blank and zero as different things or you'll flag real customers.

Signal 6 of 6

No email address at all

A cart that reached checkout without ever capturing an email.

Why it points to automation

Checkout asks for an email early. A cart that skipped it never got a recovery message, and probably never had a person filling in the form.

Ruled out. Every single cart in this data has an email address. Nothing to flag.
