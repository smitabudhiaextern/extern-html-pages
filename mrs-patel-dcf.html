Meet Mrs. Patel

Mrs. Patel runs a lemonade stand on Main Street. It's small. It's profitable. And it's predictable. We're going to use her stand to learn the three pillars of any DCF.

Three pillars: project the cash → add terminal value → discount back to today.
Pillar 1

Pillar 2

Add Terminal Value

The five years we just projected aren't the whole story. The stand keeps running after Year 5. It keeps making lemonade, keeps earning $8K a year. So when someone buys it, they're paying for everything that happens after Year 5 too, not just the five years on paper.

That “everything after Year 5” lump sum is called the terminal value. It's the price tag for the business once our detailed forecast runs out.

One simple way to size it: look at what similar stands sell for. If similar stands sell for 3× their annual cash, that gives us a starting price tag. Mrs. Patel's stand earns $8K a year, so 3× = $24K of terminal value at the end of Year 5.
Pillar 3

Bring the Cash Back to Today

$8,000 next year is not worth the same as $8,000 today. Today's $8K can be invested, earn a return, or sit in the bank earning interest. Next year's $8K has to wait, and waiting carries risk and opportunity cost.

So in finance, we discount future cash: we shrink each year's cash to reflect what it's actually worth today. Before we do that, let's see the naive answer first — what happens if we just add up all five years at face value.

Pillar 3, Continued

Present Value at 12%

Discounting is simple division. Take each year's cash and divide it by (1 + rate)years out. The further out the cash, the bigger the divisor, and the smaller the present value. We'll use a 12% rate as a placeholder for now.

Wrap-Up

What a DCF Does

A DCF estimates what a business is worth today by projecting its future cash, adding a terminal value for everything after, and discounting it all back to today's dollars.
