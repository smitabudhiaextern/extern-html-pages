You know Wikipedia.
Now meet Wikimedia.

You have used Wikipedia. You have almost certainly never heard of Wikimedia. They are not the same thing.

Start here. Tick anything you have done in the last week. There is no wrong answer and nothing is scored.

All three are Wikimedia. Not just the first one.

Commons is a different project. The chatbot learned from Wikipedia because Wikipedia is one of the cleanest, most cited, most multilingual things on the internet, so almost every AI model is trained on it.

So you have been using Wikimedia for years. You have just never been told its name. Everything on this page is a screenshot of a real page, so you can go and check any of it yourself.

1

It was supposed to be the other one

Two free encyclopedias grew out of the same project. The serious one went online first, in March 2000.

It wanted writers who really knew their subject, and the people who checked their work had to have a PhD, the highest degree a university can give. Every article was checked, one step at a time, before anybody was allowed to read it. That checking is called peer review. Over 2,000 people had joined its mailing list, and it had a full-time editor-in-chief running it.

Ten months later, in January 2001, it started a small side project so people could rough out drafts before the checking began.

Take a guess. After a whole year, how many finished articles did the serious one have?

Twenty one. It was called Nupedia. Worth knowing: Wikipedia's own two write-ups of it disagree, one says 21 articles in the first year and another says 12. Either way it worked out at roughly one finished article every two or three weeks. In its whole life it managed 24 approved articles, with another 150 stuck waiting to be checked.

Now look at what Nupedia's own homepage said about the side project it had started, in a sidebar, under Some Websites of Interest.

"Wiki-based fun project"

Here is that side project, in the same month.

21 articles against 18,000 in the same first year. The experts lost to the strangers, badly, and it was not close. Nupedia's server crashed in 2003 and nobody ever brought it back. The fun project now gets nearly 15 billion views a month.

3

Wikipedia has been saying that in its logo the whole time

You have seen this ten thousand times. Two things about it are deliberate, and almost nobody notices either one. Look at it properly for a second, then answer.

So what is deliberate about it?

1. The pieces at the top never arrive. The globe is unfinished on purpose, because the encyclopedia is. Every article nobody has written yet is one of those gaps. Twenty five years in, it is still not full.

2. Every piece is a different writing system. Greek, Japanese, Hebrew, Devanagari, Chinese, Cyrillic. Wikipedia was never one encyclopedia translated into other languages. It is over 300 of them, each writing its own, with its own arguments and its own gaps.

So the logo is not decoration. It is the pitch: unfinished, and not in English.

Now read the bottom of the page

Scroll to the foot of wikipedia.org and there is one small sentence doing an enormous amount of work.

"a range of other projects"

There it is. Wikipedia is not the whole thing. It is the famous one. And whoever wrote that footer is a separate organisation with a separate name.

Two names to hold on to, because they come back at the end. The organisation is the Wikimedia Foundation. The people who actually write the articles are unpaid volunteers, and nobody employs them.

6

So which of these is not a Wikimedia project?

Pick one. There is no penalty and no score.

WikiLeaks. Nothing to do with Wikimedia. Neither is wikiHow, and neither is Fandom.

"Wiki" is not a brand. It is the Hawaiian word for quick, borrowed in 1995 as a name for any website a group can edit together. Plenty of things sound like Wikimedia and are not.

The one you have used without knowing it

You may have ticked this at the top. Wikimedia Commons is the shared shelf, and every picture in every Wikipedia in every language is pulled out of it.

146,520,424

freely usable photos, videos, drawings and sounds, sitting in one pile that anyone is allowed to take from.

That was the count on 25 August 2026. It climbs every hour, so by the time you read this it is already wrong, and higher.

That is what "free knowledge" means in practice. Not just free to read. Free to take and reuse, including by you, including for something you get paid for.

So here it is plainly

The one most people get wrong is the third. Deciding what counts as a reliable source is not the Foundation's call. It is a guideline the volunteers wrote and maintain themselves, and it says so on its own page: substantive edits to it "should reflect consensus".

Three separate things, and each one does a job the other two do not.

The volunteers

  • Write and rewrite every article
  • Decide what counts as a reliable source
  • Decide what is worth an article at all
  • Settle their own arguments, in public

Do notOwn the servers, the money or the name. Nobody pays them.

The other projects

  • Hold the pictures, sounds and video
  • Hold the dictionary, the quotes, the textbooks
  • Hold the database the search panels read from
  • Lend all of it to Wikipedia, free

Do notGet any of the credit. You have used them and never known.

The Foundation

  • Runs and pays for the servers
  • Employs the lawyers and fights the legal battles
  • Owns and protects the trademarks
  • Hands out grants to volunteers

Do notWrite a single article, or decide what goes in one.

That third column is the one people get wrong. In the Foundation's own words:

"The Wikimedia Foundation generally does not write, edit, or determine what content is included on Wikipedia or how that content is maintained."

Wikimedia Foundation, Frequently Asked Questions

And it is not a small organisation being modest. Look at the size of it against the crowd it supports.

Four hundred dots. The single dark one is the Foundation's entire staff: 647, as reported on 31 December 2024. Every pale dot is about 660 of the nearly 265,000 volunteers the Foundation counted, checked 26 August 2026. Roughly one member of staff for every four hundred volunteers.

If you looked closely at the projects screenshot two sections up, it says "over 260,000 users" instead. Both numbers are Wikimedia's own, from two different pages. Neither page says what it is counting, so from the outside there is no way to tell whether they mean the same people. The ratio lands in the same place either way. Spotting a gap like that, and saying so instead of picking whichever number suits you, is the habit this externship is trying to build.

That is the whole distinction. The Foundation pays the bills. The volunteers make the rules.

One last thing

Remember that gap at the top of the globe. A logo that admits it is not finished is not an apology, it is an invitation.

Every article you have ever read has an edit button on it. It always has. The entry price is one typo.
