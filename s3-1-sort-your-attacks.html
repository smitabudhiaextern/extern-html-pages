Six Attacks, Six Families

In the Attack Lab you broke into the Grand Marina six different ways. That felt like a lot. It wasn't. Attackers know thousands of tricks, and nobody can memorize thousands.

So security people did something smart. They noticed that every attack ever invented falls into one of six families. Learn the six families and you can sort any attack, even one you have never seen before. The six-family checklist is called STRIDE. Each letter is one family.

Here is the good news: you already know five of the six families. You ran them yourself in the Attack Lab. Let's meet them properly.

Family 1 of 6
You did this

In the Attack Lab you sent fake readings that pretended to come from a Watcher. The Postman believed you, and the Lookout showed your lies as truth.

S
Spoofing
Pretending to be someone or something you're not.
The family is bigger than one attack

A fake login page that looks exactly like the Lookout. An operator types their password, and it goes straight into the attacker's hands. Same family, different trick: something fake wearing a trusted face.

The pro's question
How do we check that devices and people are who they say they are?
Family 2 of 6
You did this

In your Man-in-the-Middle attack you caught a reading mid-flight and changed it before it reached the Postman. The truth left the Watcher. A lie arrived.

T
Tampering
Changing data or settings without permission.
The family is bigger than one attack

Quietly editing the Logbook history so a leak never shows up in the records. Nobody notices data that was changed after the fact.

Your Replay attack lives in this family too. Sending an old real message again messes with the truth of "now". Remember that for the sorting game.

The pro's question
How do we know data hasn't been changed on the way?
Family 3 of 6
The one you haven't run

Five families match attacks you ran. This one is different. Nothing in the Attack Lab covered it, so meet it fresh.

R
Repudiation
Someone does something, and there is no record that can prove who did it.
At the Grand Marina

Someone triggers the Big Red Button and shuts off water to the whole hotel. Then they say "it wasn't me". If there is no log, nobody can prove anything.

Remember the $4.2M leak? When the alert was ignored, the first question was: who saw the alert, and when? Without records, that question has no answer.

The pro's question
Can we prove who did what, and when?
Family 4 of 6
You did this

In your Eavesdropping attack you sat on the guest WiFi and read the water readings flying past. You never touched a thing. You just watched.

I
Information Disclosure
Data that should be private gets seen.
The family is bigger than one attack

A forgotten backup of the whole database sitting on a public server, readable by anyone who finds the link.

Information leaks are usually step one of a bigger attack. First they learn. Then they strike.

The pro's question
Who can see this data, and who should?
Family 5 of 6
You did this

In your Denial of Service attack you flooded the Postman with junk until real messages couldn't get through. The system was still there. It just stopped working for everyone who needed it.

D
Denial of Service
Making the system unavailable to the people who need it.
The family is bigger than one attack

Locking out every operator account with failed login attempts, right in the middle of an emergency.

The scariest version: a Denial of Service during a real leak, at the exact moment someone needs the Big Red Button.

The pro's question
What happens if this part goes down?
Family 6 of 6
You did this

In your Unauthorized Access attack you got in where you didn't belong. That first small foothold was the whole point.

E
Elevation of Privilege
Starting with a little access and growing it into a lot.
The family is bigger than one attack

Someone with view-only access to the Lookout finds a bug that lets them send valve commands. Or they hop from the water system into the hotel's payment network.

The pro's question
If someone gets a little access, can they turn it into a lot?

The Second Tool

Sorting Tells You What Can Happen. Not What to Fix First.

For that, professionals score every threat with two questions:

How likely is it?
Low · Medium · High
How bad would it be?
Low · Medium · High · Critical
Likelihood × Impact = Risk

Notice the mismatch: likelihood has 3 levels, impact has 4. Impact gets an extra level, Critical, because some failures are so catastrophic that "high" isn't a big enough word for them.
