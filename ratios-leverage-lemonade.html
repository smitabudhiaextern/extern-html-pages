01 / 08 · COVER
Before the ratios — the idea behind them
What is leverage, really?

Leverage is borrowed money used to grow the business faster than you could with just your own savings. Maya had $500 of her own money. She borrowed $1,500 more to reach a scale she couldn't have reached alone.

Without the loan
Maya buys a $500 cart. Sells to the corner, maybe one café. Same business as last summer.
With the loan
Maya buys a $2,000 truck. Delivers to 5 cafés. Revenue jumps from $1,500 to $10,000.

Leverage amplifies returns when things go well. It also amplifies losses when they don't — that $120 of yearly interest has to be paid whether Maya sells 100 cups or 10,000. Leverage ratios measure how much of that amplification a business is carrying.

Debt / EBITDA

Now that you've seen what leverage is, the first question lenders ask is the simplest: how big is the debt compared to the cash the business throws off each year? Debt over EBITDA gives you that number in one ratio.

Debt / EBITDA = Total Debt ÷ EBITDA
= $1,500 ÷ $3,000
0.5xDebt / EBITDA
What this tells you: About 6 months of EBITDA would pay the loan off in full. Most lenders get nervous above 4–5x for a small business.
Try one: If Maya took another $4,500 loan to add a second truck route, new Debt / EBITDA? 2.0x — $6,000 ÷ $3,000
03 / 08 · DEBT / EBITDA
Ratio 2 of 3 · Can she actually pay the interest?

Interest Coverage

Debt / EBITDA told you the size of the loan. Interest Coverage tells you whether Maya can comfortably pay the interest bill — $120 a year on her $1,500 loan at 8%. The formula compares the profit she earns from operating (EBIT) to the interest she owes.

Heads up: Lenders often look at this before Debt / EBITDA. If a business can't cover the interest, none of the other numbers matter — the loan is in trouble immediately.

The same loan can look bigger or smaller depending on cash on hand.

Net Debt / EBITDA · the cash you already have counts.

Interest coverage looked fine. Now think about Maya's $500 in the bank. That cash could pay debt down tomorrow if she wanted, so the real debt burden is smaller than the top-line ratio. Net Debt / EBITDA subtracts cash before doing the math.

Highly leveraged businesses look brilliant in growth years and dangerous in recessions. That's why analysts stress-test — they re-run the ratios under a bad scenario before they trust the top-line ratio. EBITDA falls faster than revenue here because fixed costs (truck, insurance, permits) don't shrink when sales do.

06 / 08 · STRESS TEST
Before you apply these to a real company

How to judge whether a leverage ratio is "safe" or "stretched"

01
Compare to industry
Utilities sit comfortably at 3–5x Debt / EBITDA because cash flows are steady. Cyclicals (autos, oil) get nervous above 2x. SaaS often runs with zero debt. Same number, very different meaning.
02
Check both gross and net
A company at 3x gross / 1x net is in a different place than one at 3x gross / 3x net. The cash balance changes the story. Run both before you decide.
03
Stress-test the coverage
Ask: at what revenue decline does interest coverage drop below 2x? That number is the real survival question — and the one most worth knowing before you write a memo.

Apply this same framework to every leverage ratio you compute on your real company next. Number + context + stress test tells you everything; the number alone tells you nothing.
