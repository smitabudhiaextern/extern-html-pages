The AI Data Center Energy Surge

Why the buildings that run artificial intelligence need so much electricity, and why that pulls natural gas into the story.
For a decade, data centers barely used more power even as computing exploded. Then AI arrived, and power demand broke sharply upward. Operators are racing to feed it several ways, building their own on-site generation and restarting nuclear plants to skip the multi-year grid queue. But natural gas is the most important of them, expected to supply about 60% of the new demand.

1 What it is

AI data centers are buildings full of computers that train and run artificial intelligence. The issue is simple: they need far more electricity than older data centers, and far more of them are being built.

The reason is density. One modern AI server rack (an NVIDIA GB200 NVL72) draws about 120 kW, roughly 6 to 10 times a traditional rack's 10–20 kW. AI involves two kinds of work: training, which teaches the model and is very power-heavy, and inference, which runs the finished model for users and is lighter per rack but happens at huge volume.

2 How we got here

Through the 2010s, data-center power use stayed nearly flat even as computing exploded, because efficiency improved just as fast. U.S. data-center electricity rose only about 4% from 2010 to 2014. Then AI broke the trend.

2010–2014: Power use nearly flat (+4%) despite booming compute. Efficiency kept pace. (LBNL, 2016)
Late 2022: ChatGPT launches. The AI boom arrives just as efficiency gains level off.
2023: U.S. data centers hit 176 TWh, or 4.4% of all U.S. electricity. (LBNL 2024 / U.S. DOE)
2028: Projected 325–580 TWh, or 6.7–12% of U.S. electricity. From here, more compute means more power.

4 Why it matters to oil & gas

Power is now the number-one constraint on building a data center, ahead of land, money, or chips. The wait to connect a new project to the U.S. grid runs a median of more than five years, with over 2,000 GW stuck in interconnection queues at the end of 2025 (Goldman Sachs, 2025). Developers need power immediately, so they are going around the queue: building their own on-site generation, signing nuclear deals, and, most of all, turning to natural gas.

Forecasters see data centers adding roughly 3 to 6 Bcf/d of new U.S. natural gas demand by 2030 in the central case, against total U.S. consumption near 90 Bcf/d. The full analyst range runs 3 to 20 Bcf/d, so this is a large but still uncertain number.

Why gas, not wind, solar, or nuclear?

Gas runs 24 hours a day on demand, and an on-site plant can be powered on in about 18–36 months, two to four times faster than waiting in the grid queue, at roughly $45–75 per MWh (Enverus, 2025). Demand is so strong that GE Vernova's gas turbines are sold out through 2030–2031, a backlog near 100 GW, and new gas-plant construction costs have jumped about 66%.

Real projects are already moving. Homer City, Pennsylvania, a retired coal plant, is becoming a 4.5 GW gas-fired data-center campus on seven GE Vernova turbines, above $10 billion, targeted around 2027, which would be the largest gas plant in the U.S. (GE Vernova, 2025). Chevron and ExxonMobil are also building gas plants dedicated to data centers.
