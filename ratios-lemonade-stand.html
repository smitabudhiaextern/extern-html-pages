01 / 09 · COVER
First — the simplest question
What's a ratio, actually?
A ratio = one thing ÷ another thing
That's it. Every ratio you'll see is just division dressed up to answer a business question — like "of every dollar Maya took in, how much was left after she paid for the lemons?" We'll work through five of them on her stand. Small numbers, no jargon, no surprises.

Gross Margin

Of every dollar of lemonade Maya sold, how much was left after the stuff that went INTO the cup? That's gross margin. It tells you whether the product itself makes money.

Gross Margin = (Revenue – COGS) ÷ Revenue
= ($1,500 – $450) ÷ $1,500 = $1,050 ÷ $1,500
70%Gross Margin
What this tells you: 70¢ of every dollar is still on the table after the lemons. Plenty of room to cover everything else.
Try one: If lemons get expensive and COGS rises to $600, what's the new GM? 60% — ($1,500 – $600) ÷ $1,500

Operating Margin

Gross margin only counted the lemons. Now add the cost of running the stand — the permit, the flyers, the cart wearing out. What's left after ALL of that is operating income, and operating margin is its share of revenue.

Operating Margin = Operating Income ÷ Revenue
= ($1,050 – $100 – $50 – $50) ÷ $1,500 = $850 ÷ $1,500
57%Operating Margin
Why this matters more than GM for forecasting: Operating margin captures the full cost of running the business. It's the line item most analysts project forward.
Try one: If Maya adds $100 of Instagram ads, what's her new OM? 50% — $750 ÷ $1,500
04 / 09 · OPERATING MARGIN
This one trips up everyone the first time. Read it twice — it'll click.

EBITDA Margin · the cart that already wore out

Operating Income subtracted $50 for the cart wearing out. But Maya didn't actually pay $50 this summer — she paid $100 for the cart last year. EBITDA adds that $50 back to show what the operating business produced before non-cash charges. (It's not cash flow — that's the next slide. EBITDA is a halfway step between Operating Income and true cash.)

What the income statement says

Revenue$1,500
– COGS($450)
– OpEx($150)
– Depreciation($50)
Operating Income$850
OM = 57%
Add depreciation back

Operating profit + non-cash charges

Operating Income$850
+ Depreciation (back)$50
EBITDA$900
EBITDA Margin = $900 ÷ $1,500 = 60%
That's the hardest concept in this whole deck. The rest builds on what you already know. EBITDA is just OM with the non-cash stuff (depreciation, amortization) added back — useful when you want to compare businesses that bought equipment in different years.
05 / 09 · EBITDA
Ratio 4 of 5 · What actually landed in her pocket

Free Cash Flow Margin

EBITDA is what flowed this year — but Maya still had to buy the cart in summer 1. Free Cash Flow subtracts that. Same business, two summers, very different cash story.

Summer 1 Bought the cart
EBITDA$900
– Cart purchase (CapEx)($100)
– Working capital change$0
Free Cash Flow$800
53%FCF Margin
Summer 2 Cart already owned
EBITDA$900
– No new CapEx$0
– Working capital change$0
Free Cash Flow$900
60%FCF Margin
That's the whole lesson: a year a company invests heavily (new cart, new factory, new servers) will show lower FCF than a year it doesn't — even if the underlying business is identical. FCF is the truest cash story.
06 / 09 · FCF MARGIN
Ratio 5 of 5 · Was the money worth putting in?

Operating Return on Capital

Maya put $50 of her own savings in. Her aunt loaned $50 more. That's $100 of invested capital — the total money funding the stand. ROIC asks: for every dollar of that, how much did the stand earn?

Heads up: Maya's ratio is going to look comically high — 850% — because a lemonade stand barely needs any capital. That's the point. Small business, tiny invested capital, huge return. Real public companies' ROIC (which uses after-tax operating profit) typically sits at 8–20%. We're showing the pre-tax version here because lemonade has no taxes — the shape of the math is the same.

07 / 09 · ROIC
Before you apply this to a real company
How to judge whether a ratio is "good" or "bad"
01
Compare to industry
A 12% gross margin is bad for software, closer to a grocery store's net margin (grocery GMs typically run 20–25%). Context first.
02
Look at the trend
Is margin going up, flat, or falling over 3 years? Direction matters more than level.
03
Ask what's driving it
Pricing power? Scale? Mix shift? A one-time event? The answer tells you if it'll hold.
Apply this framework to every ratio you compute on your real company in the next task. Number alone tells you nothing. Number + context + trend + driver tells you everything.
