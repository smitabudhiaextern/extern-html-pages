Cybersecurity Audits 101

A no-jargon walkthrough for someone who has never touched security work — built around all 5 projects of the vCISO outline.

What is a cybersecurity audit?

Imagine a friend says: "My house is totally safe, nobody could ever break in."

You don't just believe them. You go check: Are the doors actually locked? Do the windows have latches? Does anyone know who has a spare key? You look, and you write down exactly what you find.

A cybersecurity audit does the exact same thing, but for a company's computers and data instead of a house.

Someone (that's you, in this program) checks: What computers and data does the company actually have? Are they locked down? Who can get in? What happens if something breaks or gets stolen?

The company might say "we're secure." The whole job of the audit is to test that claim and come back with proof — yes or no, and exactly why.

The whole program in two words
"Prove it."

Security is what you can prove, not what you claim. Every project in this program tests one claim the client makes about themselves.

Not hacking. This program is not about breaking into systems. It's about checking, documenting, and reporting — closer to an inspector or an accountant than a "hacker."

How did this become a job?

Zoom out first for the big picture, then zoom in if you want the extra detail behind each era.

1970s–80s

Computers were rare and separate

Security mostly meant locking the room the computer sat in. Machines usually weren't connected to anything outside the building, so the biggest risk was someone walking in, not someone reaching in from far away.
1990s

Companies plug into the internet

Once computers connected to each other worldwide, a brand-new risk appeared: a stranger could reach a company's system from anywhere in the world. The first rulebooks for computer security start appearing to answer "now what do we check?"
1996–2004

Governments start requiring proof

Laws and rules start forcing companies to actually check and document their security instead of just saying they're fine. HIPAA (1996) covers health data. PCI-DSS (2004) covers payment cards. Audits stop being optional.
2010s

Big public breaches make it real

High-profile breaches (retailers, credit bureaus) show what happens when security checks get skipped or ignored — stolen customer data, lawsuits, lost trust. Ransomware also becomes a normal business risk, not just an "IT problem."
2014 & 2024

A common scorecard appears

NIST (a US standards body) publishes the Cybersecurity Framework in 2014 — a shared checklist any company, in any industry, can use to score its own security. It's updated to CSF 2.0 in 2024. This program uses that exact framework in Project 3.
Today

Most companies rent this job, part-time

A full-time security chief (a "CISO") is expensive. Most small and mid-size companies can't justify one full-time hire. So they rent the role part-time from a firm — a "virtual CISO," or vCISO. That rented role is exactly the job this whole program simulates.

Training

What a Scanner Does, and What It Gets Wrong

You've just done by hand what a scanner does at scale — it compares what's installed on every machine against the same catalogue you just used, and reports the matches. That's it, and every scanner product does the same underlying thing. What it's good at: coverage, consistency, speed. What it's bad at: knowing which of those matches actually matters to this particular company. That's exactly why every scanner reports things that turn out to be wrong — expected, not a defect.

Sample — guess before you're told
Question
What percent of its systems does a large, well-run company have patched?
Most people guess
95–100%
Reality
100% is the theoretical goal. 90% is doing well. Plenty of large, well-run organizations sit at 60–70%.
Why
Outage windows, old software depending on other old software, suppliers who won't support the newer version, and systems nobody dares touch

Which is why a finding never recommends "patch everything." The realistic ask is always which ones, and by when.

Training

Likelihood, Impact, and Why Zero Isn't an Option

Every risk gets scored two ways: how likely it is to actually happen, and how much damage it would do if it did. Multiply the two together (in your head, not on a calculator) and you get a rough sense of how much this risk deserves your attention. Here's the part that surprises people: you can never make a risk disappear completely. You can only make it smaller, hand the cost of it to someone else, or decide to live with it. A recommendation to "eliminate all risk" is a sign the person writing it doesn't understand the job — it's not an achievable state for any real company.

Sample — scoring one finding, and defending it
Finding
Outdated encryption setting on the patient billing database server, on the federal government's actively-exploited list (Project 2)
Likelihood
High — it's on a public list of flaws attackers are exploiting right now, not a theoretical one
Impact
High — this is the same server Project 1 scored Confidentiality: High, Integrity: High

You're not running a formula here — you're making a judgment call you can defend out loud. A simple scale you can explain in one sentence beats an elaborate one you can't.

Training

Accept, Mitigate, Transfer, Avoid

Once a risk is scored, there are exactly four things you can do about it — no fifth option exists. You'll use these four words for the rest of the project, so this module is about knowing them cold.

Mitigate

Reduce it — by fixing the underlying problem or adding a control. Example: patching the outdated encryption setting on the billing database server.

Transfer

Move the cost to someone else — through insurance, or a contract clause that puts liability on a vendor. Example: cyber insurance covering the cost of a breach involving the forgotten portal.

Avoid

Stop doing the thing that creates the risk. Example: decommissioning the forgotten 2019 portal entirely instead of patching it, since nobody uses it anyway.

Accept

Decide to live with it, formally, on the record. Not a shrug — a valid acceptance names an owner, gives a written reason, and sets a review date. Without those three things, it's not an acceptance. It's just something nobody got around to.

Which of your findings is a genuine candidate for acceptance? Hold that question — Step 2 of this project puts one specific finding through exactly that decision, in front of the person who controls the budget.
