Stop 1 · The Hook

What does competition really mean?

Imagine you're trying to pick a place to eat. You're craving burgers, so you check out McDonald's — but before ordering, you quickly compare it to Burger King. Why? Because you want the best burger for your money. 🍔

That's competition.

Before you dive in, let's get one thing straight: who counts as a competitor, really?

💡 Quick Think

Before you read on — what do you think makes two companies "competitors"? Take 10 seconds. Jot it in your head. Then reveal the working definition.

Two companies are competitors when a customer might choose one instead of the other. It's not about whether they sell the same thing on a shelf. It's about whether they're fighting for the same customer's decision.

Stop 3 · Two Kinds

Direct vs. indirect competition

Not all competitors compete the same way. There are two main types — and learning to tell them apart is the actual skill.

Direct competition

Same product, same customer, head-to-head

Spotify vs. Apple Music. Both stream music. Both target the same listeners. When you decide where to listen, you're picking one or the other — not both.

Direct competition is when businesses sell similar products to the same customers. A customer choosing one means they're not choosing the other.

Indirect competition

Different products, same underlying need

Netflix vs. YouTube. Netflix sells curated shows and movies. YouTube serves up creator vlogs and how-tos. Different products. But both compete for the same thing — your screen time.

Indirect competition happens when different products compete to satisfy the same customer need, even if they're not selling the same thing.

Stop 5 · The Framework

How to identify direct competitors

When you sit down to find competitors for your selected company, run every candidate through these three questions. If the answer is yes to all three, it's a direct competitor.

1 Do they offer the same core product or service?

Alight Solutions provides HR, payroll, and benefits outsourcing.

Direct competitors: Accolade, Fidelity, Paychex.
Not direct: Microsoft — sells business software, but not HR outsourcing.

2 Do they target the same customers?

If two companies both sell to large enterprises, they're direct competitors. If one focuses on small businesses and the other on Fortune 500s, they may not be — even if the product looks similar.

3 Are customers actively choosing between them for the same solution?

If customers are comparing two companies to solve the same problem with similar products or services — direct competitors. If the companies offer different solutions that fulfill the same broader need (entertainment, productivity, getting around) — indirect competitors.
