Step 1 of 5

Free Cash Flow

The cash the business actually generates each year.

Profit isn't cash. It includes non-cash items (like depreciation) and ignores real cash that went out (like CapEx). Free Cash Flow strips those out — the actual cash a business throws off, before paying lenders or shareholders. Four terms, each fixes one accounting-vs-cash gap.

  • NOPAT — operating profit after cash tax. The closest income-statement line to real operating cash. ALIT $530M
  • + D&A — non-cash charge, added back. The equipment's already owned; depreciation just spreads its cost across years. ALIT $200M
  • − CapEx — real cash going out for buildings, equipment, software. ALIT $150M
  • − ΔWC — cash tied up in growth: inventory, receivables. $50M more WC means $50M less FCF. ALIT $50M

This is unlevered FCF — no interest subtracted because we're valuing the whole business, not just equity.

Step 2 of 5

Forecast the next 5 years

One year of FCF (Free Cash Flow) isn't a valuation. We need a future.

A business is worth the cash it generates over many years — not just last year's number.

So we project FCF forward. Most analysts go 5 to 10 years. Past that, the future gets too speculative to model line-by-line.

The growth rate you pick matters. It's grounded in three things:

  • 1. History — how fast the business actually grew
  • 2. Sector — how fast the industry is growing
  • 3. Guidance — what management has said about the future

Analysts argue about this number for hours.

Heads up. This annual growth rate applies to the forecast window only. We use a different growth rate — much lower — for what happens after Year 5. That's the next step.

Step 3 of 5 — Concept

Terminal Value — why it exists

What's the company worth AFTER your forecast ends?

Your forecast ends at Year 5. ALIT doesn't. It keeps generating cash for as long as it exists — potentially forever. Terminal Value (TV) captures all that cash beyond Year 5, collapsed into a single number.

We can't model Year 6, 7, 50, 200 individually. So we make one simplifying assumption:

After Year 5, FCF grows at a steady, modest rate — forever.

That rate is the perpetual growth rate (g).

Where does g come from?

g is tied to long-run GDP and inflation — typically 2–3% in developed markets. The logic: no company can grow faster than the economy forever, or it would eventually BE the economy. So g has a hard ceiling. For ALIT, we use g = 2%.

Step 3 of 5 — Math

Terminal Value — the formula

The Gordon Growth Model — valuing an infinite stream of cash.

Once FCF grows at g forever, math gives a closed-form answer for the infinite stream:

TV = FCFfinal × (1 + g) / (WACC − g)
  • FCFfinal — final-year forecast FCF
  • g — perpetual growth rate (2–3%)
  • WACC — the rate investors demand (Step 4)
The intuition

If $X next year grows at g forever, today's value is X / (WACC − g) — the growing-perpetuity formula. The (1+g) bumps this year's FCF to next year's.

WACC must be greater than g. Otherwise the denominator goes negative and the formula breaks. As g approaches WACC, TV explodes — try the slider.

Step 4 of 5

Discount everything to today

$100 next year isn't worth $100 today.

Money has time value. A dollar today beats a dollar next year — invest the dollar today, you'd have $1.08 (at an 8% return). To compare future cash to today's dollars, we discount each cash flow back to its Present Value (PV):

PV = Future Cash / (1 + WACC)Year

The discount rate is WACC — Weighted Average Cost of Capital: the blended return the business owes its investors (debt + equity). A higher WACC means future cash is worth less today.

Step 5 of 5

Intrinsic price per share

From a $7.9B business to a single share price.

Enterprise Value is what the whole business is worth — debt holders and equity holders together. But you're buying shares, an equity claim. So strip out debt:

Equity Value = Enterprise Value − Net Debt

Net Debt = total debt minus cash on the balance sheet. Cash partially offsets debt — the business could use it to pay debt down. Then:

Implied Price = Equity Value / Shares Outstanding

That's what your DCF says one share should be worth. Compare to the market — over, under, or fair.
