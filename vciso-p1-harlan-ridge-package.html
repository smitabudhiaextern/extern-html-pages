Security Assessment: Harlan Ridge Credit Union

Prepared by vCISO Services, LLC, for the board of Harlan Ridge Credit Union. Assessed service: member onboarding and account servicing. Engagement conducted February to April. Distribution: board, CEO, VP Operations, Keystone Financial Systems on request.

Executive summary

Harlan Ridge Credit Union runs most of the controls it believes it runs. It can prove very few of them, and almost none outside head office.

The annual examination is approaching, and the core banking vendor has asked for evidence of security practice as a condition of contract renewal. Both will ask the same kind of question: not whether a control exists, but whether it can be shown operating. Of nine security claims the credit union made in its most recent vendor questionnaire, three could be supported with evidence. The other six describe practices that mostly do happen but leave no record an examiner would accept.

The assessment identified 22 assets in the assessed service. Seven have no recorded owner and four appear on no internal record at all. It found 17 issues, which consolidate to 11 distinct risks. The three that matter most: a branch VPN appliance carrying a flaw attackers are actively using, a retired internet banking test system still reachable from the internet and belonging to nobody, and shared teller logins at four of six branches.

None of this reflects wrongdoing. The pattern is common in institutions this size: practice outran paperwork, and head office matured while the branches did not. What systems do automatically is provable; what people decide usually is not. Section 6 lists the risks in the order we suggest the board consider them. What to do about each is a decision for management, and this report stops short of making it.

Section 1 · Engagement scope and asset inventory

1.1 What this engagement covered

Harlan Ridge Credit Union engaged vCISO Services to assess the security of its member onboarding and account servicing function ahead of the annual examination and a pending request from its core banking vendor.

The assessed service covers the process by which a person becomes a member and then manages their account: identity verification at branch and online, account creation in the core banking platform, and the member-facing web and mobile channels. It includes the six branch locations, the online banking front end, and the third parties involved in identity verification and card issuance.

The engagement did not cover lending and underwriting, the ATM estate, payment processing and settlement, or corporate IT. These are scheduled separately. Where a system in one of those areas touches the assessed service, it has been recorded and flagged rather than assessed.

This was an assessment, not a penetration test. No system was tested actively, and all external observation used publicly available information only.

1.2 What was found

An asset inventory was produced for the assessed service. It had not previously existed in any form.

22 assets were identified. Seven have no recorded owner on any register the credit union maintains. Four appear on no internal record at all and were identified from the engagement brief and from vendor contracts. Two entries conflict between the IT asset register and the vendor register.

The credit union maintains two records that overlap: an IT asset register kept by the infrastructure team, and a vendor and equipment register kept by Finance for contract renewal. Neither is authoritative and they have never been reconciled. This is not unusual at this size. It is, however, the reason the first two findings exist.

1.3 Findings

F-01

Seven assets in the assessed service have no recorded owner.

What was observed
Of 22 assets identified, seven carry no named owner on the IT asset register, the vendor register, or any other record produced during the engagement. They include the identity verification service, the member document store, and the certificate covering the online banking domain.
Why it matters
An asset with no owner has nobody answerable for patching it, reviewing access to it, or retiring it, and two of the seven sit directly in the path of member identity documents. The core banking vendor’s questionnaire asks whether an asset inventory with assigned ownership is maintained; today that answer cannot be yes.
Evidence
Asset inventory rows 6, 9, 11, 14, 17, 19, 22; IT asset register, owner column; vendor register, contract owner column.
Severity
High. The gap is systemic and directly visible to the party about to ask.
F-02

The two internal registers disagree, and neither is treated as authoritative.

What was observed
The IT asset register records the member document store as decommissioned in 2023. The vendor register shows an active storage contract for the same service, invoiced monthly through the current year. Separately, the identity verification provider appears on the vendor register with an active integration and does not appear on the IT asset register at all.
Why it matters
In the first case a service believed retired may still hold member identity documents. In the second, a third party has an active connection that the team responsible for security does not know exists. Neither register is wrong on purpose; they were built by different teams for different reasons, and no process requires them to agree.
Evidence
IT asset register row 31; vendor register rows 8 and 12; storage invoices, January through October.
Severity
High for the identity verification integration, medium for the document store pending confirmation of whether it holds live data.
F-03

Three of nineteen intake questions could not be answered by the credit union.

What was observed
A standard engagement intake sheet was completed from the engagement brief and supporting documents. Three questions could not be answered from anything the credit union holds: which individual owns each system, how many external connections reach the service, and which systems hold member data.
Why it matters
A regulator or a vendor conducting due diligence would ask all three, and the honest answer to each is that the record does not exist. The third turned out to matter most: nobody could say whether the document store believed retired in 2023 still holds member identity documents, and that uncertainty became finding F-02.
Evidence
Completed intake sheet, questions 3, 11 and 12.
Severity
Medium. Each gap is fixable; together they describe a service run from memory rather than records.

Section 2 · External exposure

2.1 Method

Everything in this section was observed from outside, using public sources only: the certificate transparency logs, DNS records, the public grading tools for transfer encryption and browser security headers, and the public breach-notification database. Nothing was sent at any credit union system. This is what any outsider can see, which is why it was measured.

2.2 Findings

The certificate transparency logs return eleven hostnames issued under harlanridgecu.org over nine years. Eight are current and accounted for. Three are not: ib-test, enroll-old, and branchmail. Two no longer resolve. One does.

F-04

A retired internet banking test system is still reachable and belongs to nobody.

What was observed
The hostname ib-test.harlanridgecu.org still resolves and still serves a login page for an internet banking product whose vendor contract ended in 2022. Nobody at the credit union could say which machine serves it, who pays for it, or why it survived the migration. It is not on the asset inventory, so it is also outside the quarterly scan.
Why it matters
A login page for a defunct product, carrying the credit union’s name, on infrastructure nobody owns, is what an attacker looks for first and an examiner asks about second.
Evidence
Certificate transparency log extract; DNS resolution on the assessment date; screenshot of the served page.
Severity
High.
F-05

The public marketing site is weakly configured and collects member email addresses.

What was observed
The site at harlanridgecu.org is operated by an outside creative agency on shared hosting. It scores D on the browser security-header grade, one legacy path does not redirect to the encrypted site, and its newsletter form feeds member email addresses to a third-party mailing service nobody at the credit union has assessed.
Why it matters
The site holds no account data, but it is the credit union’s public face and the mailing list it feeds is a member list.
Evidence
Header grading report; redirect check; the agency contract, which contains no security requirements.
Severity
Medium.
F-06

Email protection is configured but set to monitor only, and nobody reads the monitor.

What was observed
The credit union publishes both standard email protection records. The enforcement record has been set to take no action for nineteen months, and the reports it generates go to a mailbox nobody checks.
Why it matters
The system that could reject forged mail sent in the credit union’s name is present and enforcing nothing. Members act on emails that appear to come from Harlan Ridge.
Evidence
Public DNS records; the unread report mailbox, confirmed with the infrastructure team.
Severity
Medium, raised from low because members are retail account holders.
F-07

Three staff email addresses appear in third-party breach data.

What was observed
Public breach records show three staff addresses exposed in breaches of other companies’ services, one within the last year. One of the three was used to register the marketing site’s content system, and the agency confirmed that password has not been changed since registration.
Why it matters
Someone else’s breach becomes Harlan Ridge’s problem the day a password is reused, and that cannot be verified from outside.
Evidence
Public breach database results; content-system account list from the agency.
Severity
Medium, contingent on reuse.

One thing was checked and found in good order, and it belongs in this report as much as the findings do. The online banking front end, hosted by the digital banking vendor, scores A on both public grades, enforces encrypted connections throughout, and exposes nothing it should not. Member-facing banking is the best-configured thing the credit union has. The weaknesses are in what grew up around it.

Section 3 · Vulnerabilities and patching

3.1 What the scan covers, and what it cannot see

The infrastructure team runs a quarterly vulnerability scan and runs it on schedule; the most recent completed three weeks before this assessment. That discipline is real. The scope is the problem: the scan covers head office only. The six branches, where members are onboarded and served, have never been scanned.

F-08

The vulnerability scan has never included the branches.

What was observed
Six branch networks, containing teller workstations, check scanners and branch network equipment, sit outside the scan and always have.
Why it matters
The scan reports 31 findings at head office. The number at the branches is not low; it is unknown, and those are different things.
Evidence
Scan configuration; scope discussion with the infrastructure team.
Severity
High. The blind spot covers the assessed service’s primary workplaces.

3.2 The 31 rows, read

The head-office scan returned 31 rows, which resolve to six distinct issues once grouped by cause. Four are material, ranked here by what they would cost the business rather than by the scanner’s own scoring. The remaining two are housekeeping items on the print infrastructure, recorded in appendix C.

F-09

The branch VPN concentrator runs firmware two releases behind, and one of its flaws is being exploited in the wild.

What was observed
The appliance connecting all six branches to head office runs firmware last updated two years ago. One flaw in that version appears on the public catalogue of vulnerabilities confirmed as actively exploited.
Why it matters
This is the highest-priority technical item in the report: the device is reachable from the internet, it is the door to every branch, and the flaw is not theoretical.
Evidence
Scan rows 4 through 9, grouped; the exploited-vulnerabilities catalogue entry.
Severity
High.
F-10

The teller check-scanning software requires a browser runtime its maker stopped fixing.

What was observed
All six branches run check-scanning software that works only inside an out-of-support browser runtime.
Why it matters
The vendor’s current version removes the dependency; the credit union is two versions behind on a product it pays maintenance for. Nine of the 31 scan rows are this one issue, printed once per machine.
Evidence
Scan rows, grouped; vendor release notes.
Severity
Medium.
F-11

Two head-office servers are three months behind on updates because their reboot window keeps being surrendered.

What was observed
The servers hosting the document workflow are patched in a monthly window that has been given up three months running for month-end processing.
Why it matters
The infrastructure team knows, has raised it, and has no authority to take the window back. The missing item is authority, not effort.
Evidence
Patch logs; change calendar.
Severity
Medium.
F-12

The core banking server is one version behind, and this one is fine.

What was observed
Keystone certifies its platform against specific operating system builds, and the credit union correctly runs the certified build rather than the newest one. The scan flags this as a finding.
Why it matters
It is the vendor’s documented requirement, with compensating restrictions in place and in writing. It is recorded here as an accepted risk because distinguishing a documented, accepted risk from an unmanaged one is most of what separates an assessment from a scanner export.
Evidence
Vendor certification matrix; the compensating-control note in the IT register.
Severity
Accepted risk, documented, not ranked.

Section 4 · Controls and framework position

4.1 Findings

The credit union’s Information Security Policy is reviewed annually and was updated eight months ago. On paper this is a well-governed institution. The assessment tested whether the paper reaches the branches, and mostly it does not.

F-13

Four of six branches operate shared teller logins.

What was observed
Head office enforces individual accounts with a second authentication factor for every user. At four branches, tellers share one login per station, with the password on a rotation the branch manages itself.
Why it matters
The policy requires individual accountability for every transaction. Shared credentials make that impossible, and they also mean the head-office authentication rules do not reach the branch floor. When something goes wrong at a teller station, the credit union cannot say who was at the keyboard.
Evidence
Authentication configuration; branch walkthrough notes; policy section 4.2, which the practice contradicts.
Severity
High.
F-14

Access recertification runs at head office and has never run at a branch.

What was observed
Each quarter, someone at head office reviews who holds which access, signs it, and files it. It is the best-evidenced control the credit union has. No equivalent review has ever covered branch staff, whose access accumulates as they move between roles.
Why it matters
The control exists, operates, and is provable; its boundary was drawn short of most of the workforce.
Evidence
Four quarters of signed head-office recertifications; no branch equivalent exists.
Severity
Medium.
F-15

Security alerts arrive in a mailbox with no owner and no procedure.

What was observed
The core banking vendor and the VPN appliance both send security alerts to one shared mailbox. Nobody owns it, nothing documents what happens when an alert lands, and the email-protection reports from F-06 go to the same place. At the time of the engagement the mailbox held 240 unread messages.
Why it matters
Detection nobody reads is indistinguishable from no detection.
Evidence
Mailbox configuration; unread count; no documented procedure exists.
Severity
Medium.

4.2 Inherent risk

Scored before considering any control: elevated. The credit union moves money, holds identity documents and account records for 51,000 members, operates six physical locations plus internet-facing channels, and is regulated with a scheduled annual examination. Good or bad practice does not change this score.

4.3 Current and target profile

The credit union was scored against the six functions of NIST CSF 2.0, each scored 0 to 4, current state against a target justified by the inherent risk. The target is not the maximum anywhere. A 200-person credit union does not need, and could not operate, the security apparatus of a bank fifty times its size. What it needs is consistency between head office and branch, and records where there are currently memories.

Section 5 · Evidence

5.1 Method

The credit union’s most recent vendor questionnaire, completed for Keystone eleven months ago, makes nine security claims. Each was tested the same way: find the artifact that shows the claim operating, on a date, done by someone. A claim scored as supported only when that artifact could be handed over today.

5.2 The scoreboard

Three of nine claims are supported. Onboarding identity verification leaves complete records, automatically, for every new member. The annual policy review has minutes and a sign-off page. The quarterly head-office scan produces dated reports going back years. Two of the three are things systems do automatically; the third is a formal meeting with an agenda. Systems and meetings leave records. Most of the credit union’s other security work happens between people, and it shows.

The six unsupported claims follow a pattern, not a scandal. Staff security training happens annually; sign-in sheets exist for two of the last three years. Departing staff lose access the same day, according to everyone asked, and no ticket or record marks any individual departure. Access reviews happen quarterly at head office only, as in F-14. The disaster recovery test performed in 2023 was real; the report it was meant to produce was never written, so as evidence it does not exist. Logs are kept twelve months according to policy; on the branch appliances the actual retention is thirty days, and nobody had checked. Due diligence on major vendors is thorough; on the agency that runs the public website and the mailing service behind it, there is none.

F-16

Six of nine prior security claims cannot be evidenced, and the failures cluster where work happens between people.

What was observed
Nobody lied on the Keystone questionnaire. The person who signed it believed every answer, with reason: most of the claimed practices genuinely occur.
Why it matters
What the credit union lacks is not practice but proof, and it lacks proof exactly where no system generates it automatically.
Evidence
The nine-claim scoreboard, appendix B, with the tested artifact or its absence noted per claim.
Severity
High in aggregate. The examiner and the vendor will both apply this same test.
F-17

The log retention the policy promises is not what the branches deliver.

What was observed
Policy says twelve months. Head office complies. The branch network appliances retain thirty days.
Why it matters
For most of the credit union’s physical footprint, any investigation older than a month starts with nothing. The appliances shipped with a default and nobody revisited it.
Evidence
Policy section 7.1; appliance configuration exports.
Severity
Medium.

Section 6 · Consolidated risk picture

The seventeen findings consolidate to eleven distinct risks. F-12 is an accepted, documented risk and is not ranked. Five pairs of the remaining sixteen turned out to be the same problem observed from two directions: unowned assets and the live retired system are one risk; the two register conflicts and the unanswerable intake questions are one risk; shared teller logins and the branch recertification gap are one; silent email enforcement and the unowned alert mailbox are one; the weak marketing site and the stale breach-exposed credential on its content system are one.
