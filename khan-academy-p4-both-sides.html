The benefit line: five rounds

Soon you will write the both sides field for two deals of your own. This is where you work out what a good benefit line looks like first. Five rounds. Two versions of the same benefit line, for the same partner, and you press the one you think is stronger.

Two words first. A value proposition is the plain answer to one question: what does each side actually get out of this. A unit is the thing a benefit is counted in. Learners. Dollars. Compute hours. Staff hours saved. Schools reached.

Every line you are about to read either has a unit on it or does not, and which one it is turns out to matter more than how it sounds.

The reason appears either way, whichever one you press, so there is nothing to lose by guessing. Nothing here is scored.

Five checks to write your own against

One line each, and each one names a way a benefit line fails. Nothing you write next is scored either.

1 Exposure is not a benefit, it is a hope Say what the partner can do with those learners. Being seen is not a thing anybody can approve.
2 A benefit with no unit cannot be sized Name the unit it arrives in. The estimate you write later in this step can only count a unit that was named here.
3 Write it from their chair, not yours If the line only says what Khan Academy needs, it has not yet said why this partner is the one.
4 Alignment and value are not benefits They are labels you put on a benefit once you have one. Cross out the abstract nouns and see what is left.
5 It has to beat what they already do Name the thing they do today. If its cost is not published, write that, and write where you looked.
