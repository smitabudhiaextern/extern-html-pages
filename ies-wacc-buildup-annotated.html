How the IES WACC Build-Up Works

The IES model uses a discount rate of 16.25% to value the business. That single number is built by adding five pieces together. This page explains each piece: what it is, where the number comes from, and what value IES used.

1

Risk-free rate

What it is
The return you can earn from a safe investment, like lending money to the US government. It's the starting point. Any investment that carries real risk should pay more than this.
Where to pull it
Use the current yield on the 10-year US Treasury bond. You can look it up at treasury.gov. The 10-year version matches the medium-term horizon a DCF projects over, and it's the most liquid benchmark on the curve, so it's the standard reference point in valuation work.
Why IES has 4.25%
When the IES model was built, the 10-year Treasury yield was around 4.25%. If you were running this model today, you'd update this with whatever the current rate is.

Equity risk premium

What it is
Investors won't put money in a stock unless it pays more than a safe Treasury. The equity risk premium is the extra return they demand for taking on stock-market risk on top of the risk-free rate.
Where to pull it
Kroll publishes a recommended equity risk premium every quarter. Their latest number is the standard reference for US-based valuations. Pull it from Kroll's cost of capital page.
Why IES has 5.50%
This was Kroll's most recent recommendation when the IES model was built. Kroll updates the number every quarter, so it would be different if you ran the model today.

Size premium

What it is
Small companies are riskier than large ones. They have less ability to absorb a bad year, less access to credit, less negotiating power. Investors demand extra return for owning a smaller company, on top of the standard equity risk premium. That extra is the size premium.
Where to pull it
Kroll publishes a size premium for each decile of company size (decile 1 = largest, decile 10 = smallest). The smallest companies get the highest premium. The Kroll Cost of Capital Navigator is a paid product; if you don't have access, the historical range for the smallest deciles is roughly 3-6%.
Why IES has 3.50%
IES Communications, treated as a small standalone segment, falls into one of Kroll's smaller micro/small-cap deciles. 3.50% was the size premium for that decile when the model was built.

Industry risk premium

What it is
Some industries are riskier than others. Communications infrastructure and IT services rise and fall with enterprise tech spending: they grow when customers invest in networks, data centers, and connectivity, and slow when capital budgets tighten. The industry risk premium adds extra return to reflect that built-in cyclicality.
Where to pull it
Aswath Damodaran publishes industry-specific risk premia annually on the NYU Stern data page. IT services and infrastructure firms typically fall in the 0.5% to 1.5% range. Kroll also publishes industry data for paid subscribers.
Why IES has 1.00%
IES Communications is in the communications and IT infrastructure services space. Enterprise tech-spending cycles push them above the lowest-risk industries (consumer staples, utilities) but well below high-volatility sectors like commodities or biotech. A 1% add reflects that.

Company-specific risk

What it is
An extra cushion the analyst adds for risks specific to this business that the other four components don't capture: customer concentration, key-person dependence, sub-scale operations, unusual contract terms. This one isn't published anywhere. It's the analyst's judgment call.
Where to pull it
You decide. There's no website to pull this from. The common range is 0% to 5%, and whatever value you pick, you need to justify it with the actual business risks you can name. If you can't list them, the number is probably wrong.
Why IES has 2.00%
The IES segment carries above-average customer concentration and depends on a relatively small operating team. A 2% add reflects those risks without being heavy-handed.

The total

Add the five pieces together: 4.25% + 5.50% + 3.50% + 1.00% + 2.00% = 16.25%. That's the rate the IES DCF uses to discount every future dollar back to today. A higher rate would shrink each future dollar more, lowering the valuation. A lower rate would do the opposite.

A note on debt.

When debt is zero, WACC = cost of equity. The IES model treats debt as zero. The build-up above is technically the cost of equity build-up — but with no debt to weight in, it doubles as WACC. The same is true for AV (a private division that doesn't carry separate debt).

Self-check

Take a moment to think before you click.

If size premium goes up, does WACC go up or down? What does that mean for the business's value today?

WACC goes up. A higher discount rate shrinks every future dollar of cash more, so the present value of the business gets smaller. Higher discount rate = lower business value.
