Read Mike's Printout — full text

Mike reorders by highlighting a printout, marking top sellers in yellow. It works at 15 products. Here is where it breaks at 99. The three rows Mike highlighted are Dwellings of Eldervale, POOP: The Game, and Sparkle Kitty Nights.

Printout (illustrative numbers). Columns: Product, Units in 90 days, Revenue, Page views.

  • Dwellings of Eldervale — 320 units — $42,000 — 9,500 page views (highlighted)
  • POOP: The Game — 410 units — $4,100 — 7,200 page views (highlighted)
  • Sparkle Kitty Nights — 180 units — $5,400 — 4,300 page views (highlighted)
  • Quokka Dash — 22 units — $660 — 6,800 page views (not highlighted)
  • Tiny Dungeons — 8 units — $240 — 5,100 page views (not highlighted)
  • Cardboard Castles — 0 units — $0 — 95 page views (not highlighted)

Question 1 — Traffic without sales

Quokka Dash got 6,800 page views, more than Sparkle Kitty, but sold only 22 copies, and Mike didn't highlight it. What's the most likely story? Correct answer: Lots of interest but few buyers, a conversion problem worth investigating. High traffic with few sales is a conversion problem (maybe price, photos, or description). This is a product to fix or promote, not kill, and Mike's printout can't see it.

Question 2 — Revenue hides AOV

A $40 game selling 100 units and a $200 game selling 20 units both earn $4,000. Mike's printout shows the same revenue for each. What is it NOT showing him? Correct answer: How much each individual sale is worth (AOV). Same revenue, very different products: one is volume, one is premium. Revenue alone hides AOV (dollars per unit).

Question 3 — The invisible middle and tail

Mike instantly knows his top sellers because they're loud: support tickets, restock requests, BoardGameGeek threads. Which part of the 99-product catalog is invisible to him? Correct answer: The quiet middle and the long tail. The loud bestsellers are easy. The quiet middle and tail are where reorder mistakes hide, and that's exactly what SQL is good at surfacing.

Question 4 — Decide once and commit

Once a product falls off Mike's list, it stays off until someone in the office happens to mention it. Why is that risky? Correct answer: A quietly-growing product can be missed for months. His method decides once, then commits. A product slowly gaining steam in the tail never gets a second look.

Recap — the five questions the rest of the Project answers

  1. What's the AOV distribution — where's the revenue-per-unit cliff?
  2. Which products get traffic but don't convert? (promote, don't kill)
  3. Which products convert but get no traffic? (spend to surface them)
  4. What share of revenue is the long tail? (how much can you ignore?)
  5. Are there products that haven't sold a single unit? (e.g. Cardboard Castles — stop printing)
