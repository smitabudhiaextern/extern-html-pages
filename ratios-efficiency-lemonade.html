Picking up from profitability
Profitability told you whether each cup makes money. Efficiency asks a different question: how fast is Maya's cash moving through the business?
Cash → Inventory → Sale → Receivable → Cash
Every dollar starts as cash, becomes lemons on the shelf, becomes a cup sold, becomes an invoice waiting to be paid, and eventually becomes cash again. Efficiency ratios measure how many days that round trip takes — and how many cups Maya can sell with the same dollar of working capital.

Ratio 1 of 5 · How hard is each dollar of assets working?

Asset Turnover

Now that Maya holds real assets — a cart, inventory, customer invoices — the first efficiency question is: how much revenue did each dollar of those assets generate this year?

Asset Turnover = Revenue ÷ Total Assets
= $5,000 ÷ $500
10.0xAsset Turnover
What this tells you: every $1 of assets generated $10 of revenue. That's high because lemonade is a low-asset business — no factory, no fleet. Retail businesses run HIGH asset turnover (Walmart, Costco). Capital-heavy businesses like airlines, utilities, and even software companies (with huge cash piles and intangibles) run much lower.
Try one: If Maya buys a $500 refrigerated display, what's her new asset turnover? 5.0x — $5,000 ÷ $1,000

Ratio 5 of 5 · Put the three together

Cash Conversion Cycle

Add the days cash is stuck (inventory + waiting to be paid), then subtract the days Maya gets to hold onto cash before paying suppliers. The result is the round trip.

–6

DPO · supplier gives Maya credit 73 days
CCC = DIO + DSO – DPO = 49 + 18 – 73 = –6 days
Negative CCC is a good thing. Maya's supplier gives her 73 days to pay, but her cycle only takes 67 days from buying lemons to collecting cash. The supplier funds her business. Amazon, Dell, and Costco all run negative CCC — it's a sign of structural advantage.
05 / 08 · CCC
This one feels backwards. Read it twice — it'll click.

Days = good or bad? It depends which one.

The instinct is "more days = better." That's only true for one of these. The CCC math works because the three ratios pull in opposite directions.

You want this HIGH

DPO · 73 days
Stretching supplier payments means Farmers' Co-op is essentially lending Maya money, interest-free, every cycle. The longer she takes to pay, the more cash she gets to use elsewhere.
Higher DPO→ shrinks CCC
Opposite directions

You want these LOW

DSO · 18 days  ·  DIO · 49 days
DSO low = customers paying faster. DIO low = inventory moving off the shelf faster. Both free up cash that would otherwise sit idle as receivables or unsold stock.
Lower DSO + DIO→ shrinks CCC
The takeaway: when DPO grows faster than DSO + DIO, your CCC shrinks. That frees up cash you'd otherwise need to borrow to fund the next cycle of inventory.
06 / 08 · HIGH OR LOW
Before you apply this to a real company

How to judge whether an efficiency ratio is "good" or "bad"

Same framework you saw in profitability. A number on its own tells you nothing. Three lenses make it useful.

01
Compare to industry
Software has almost no inventory; grocers carry tons. A 30-day DIO is normal for a supermarket, alarming for a SaaS company. Context first.
02
Look at the trend
DSO climbing year over year is a warning sign — customers are paying slower, often because the company is letting weaker buyers in to hit sales targets.
03
Ask what's driving it
A new big customer on bad terms? Suppliers tightening up? Inventory built up ahead of a launch? The story behind the number matters more than the number.
