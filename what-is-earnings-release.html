Every quarter starts with 5 questions.

When an earnings release drops, an analyst doesn't read it cover-to-cover. They open it looking for answers to 5 specific questions. Each section of the release is built to answer one of them.

Learn the questions, and you'll know where to look every time.

  1. How did the company perform financially this quarter?
  2. How much profit did the company make for each share?
  3. What did the company report doing this quarter?
  4. What is leadership thinking?
  5. What does the company expect next?
Question 1 of 5

"How did the company perform financially this quarter?"

Answered by
Section
Key Financial Metrics

This section includes the most important numbers that show how the company performed financially during the quarter. There are two types:

1. Standard Financial Metrics (GAAP)

  • Revenue: Total money earned from selling products or services.
  • Operating Income: Profit from regular business activities before interest and taxes.
  • Net Income: The final profit after all expenses, including taxes and interest. Often called the bottom line.

2. Non-GAAP or Industry-Specific Metrics

  • ARR (Annual Recurring Revenue): Used by subscription-based companies to show predictable revenue.
  • Adjusted EBITDA: A version of profit that excludes one-time or non-operating expenses.
What's GAAP?
GAAP (Generally Accepted Accounting Principles) is the standard set of accounting rules U.S. public companies must follow when reporting financials. Non-GAAP metrics strip out items GAAP wouldn't let you remove — like one-time costs — to show a different view of performance.
Quick Check

Is "Adjusted EBITDA" a standard GAAP metric?

Right. Adjusted EBITDA is non-GAAP — companies use it to show profit excluding one-time or non-operating items.
Question 2 of 5

"How much profit did the company make for each share an investor owns?"

Answered by
Section
EPS (Earnings Per Share)
EPS = Net Income ÷ Number of Shares Outstanding

EPS tells you how much profit the company made for each share owned by investors. Investors track EPS closely because it helps compare profitability across companies and over time.

Quick Check

A company has $200M in Net Income and 100M shares outstanding. What is EPS?

Right. $200M ÷ 100M shares = $2.00 per share.
Question 3 of 5

"What did the company report doing this quarter — what news did they make?"

Answered by
Section
Highlights from the Quarter

This section covers major events like new product launches, strategic partnerships, big contracts, or cost-saving initiatives. It adds context to the numbers and explains what may have driven strong or weak performance.

Quick Check

Which statement is more likely to appear in the Highlights section?

Right. Highlights covers the events behind the numbers — launches, partnerships, contracts. The numbers themselves live in Key Financial Metrics.
Question 4 of 5

"What is leadership thinking — and what are they focused on?"

Answered by
Section
Management Commentary

Quotes or insights from the CEO or CFO about how the company performed and what to expect next. It reveals how leadership is thinking and what they're focusing on for the future.

Quick Check

The CEO says: "We continue to see ongoing headwinds in our enterprise segment." This signals:

Right. "Headwinds" is cautious language. Read management commentary for tone, not just content — what they don't say matters too.
Question 5 of 5

"What does the company expect to happen next quarter or next year?"

Answered by
Section
Forward-Looking Guidance

The company may provide forecasts for the next quarter or full year — revenue, earnings, or other key targets. Investors care deeply about future expectations, not just past performance.

Quick Check

Why do investors often care more about forward guidance than past results?

Right. The market prices in what's already happened — future expectations are what move the stock from here.
