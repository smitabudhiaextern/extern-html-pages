Vanity or Actionable?

Six numbers from Breaking Games' data. For each one, decide whether it earns a place on Shari's dashboard. Pick an answer before you read the explanation.

ActionableIf it moves, someone decides differently

Questions 1 and 2 of 6

Would this change what Shari does?

Total Facebook pageviews: 81,653
Vanity. It's the biggest traffic number you have, which is exactly why it's tempting. But nobody can act on it. Facebook converts at 0.5%, so those 81,653 views are worth far less than the number suggests. Finish the sentence "so we should..." and you'll find you can't.
Repeat customer rate
Actionable. If it's low, you invest in winning customers back. If it's high, you protect what's working. You saw this in Project 4: only 12 shoppers ever started a second cart, which is what killed Mike's win-back email plan.

Questions 3 and 4 of 6

Would this change what Shari does?

Number of products in the catalogue: 99
Vanity. The catalogue size doesn't move. It's a fact about the store, not a signal. Nobody prints more games or fewer because the number is 99.
Share of the catalogue earning under $100: 49 of 99
Actionable. Same catalogue, but now it's a decision. Half the range barely earns anything, so Shari can ask whether those products deserve print runs, shelf space, or ad budget in Q4.

Questions 5 and 6 of 6

Would this change what Shari does?

Number of ad campaigns currently running
Vanity. Counting campaigns measures activity, not results. Running twelve campaigns is not better than running three. What matters is which ones earn their spend.
Cost per checkout session from Facebook
Actionable. This one directly sets budget. Breaking Games spent $37,201 on Meta against $61,085 of revenue, so the cost of getting one person to checkout decides whether Q4 spend goes up or down.

The category in between

Some numbers aren't actionable, but you still need them

Total revenue is the obvious one. Nobody makes a decision from $61,085 on its own. But without it, "1,972 units sold" means nothing, because you can't tell cheap-and-plenty from few-and-premium. Numbers like this are diagnostic: they give the actionable numbers their meaning.

Diagnostic numbers earn a spot on the dashboard when you pair them with the actionable number they explain.

Total revenuepairs withAverage order value
Units soldpairs withUnits per customer
Total ad spendpairs withCost per result
The rule: a diagnostic number never travels alone. On its own it's decoration. Next to the number it explains, it's context.
