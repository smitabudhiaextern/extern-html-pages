Ratio 1 of 3 · The most common acquisition multiple

EV / EBITDA

Most acquisition offers are quoted as a multiple of EBITDA. It's the cleanest way to compare two businesses, because EBITDA strips out how the company is financed and how its equipment is depreciated. First, define the two pieces — Enterprise Value (EV) is the total cost to buy the WHOLE company including its debt. EBITDA is operating profit before non-cash charges.

EV / EBITDA = Enterprise Value ÷ EBITDA
= $15,000 ÷ $3,000
5.0xEV / EBITDA
What this means: Citrus Co. saying "5x" is shorthand for "$15,000 enterprise value." Comparable small food and beverage businesses typically trade between 4x and 7x EBITDA.
Try one: If Maya grows EBITDA to $4,000 next year and the market multiple stays at 5x, what's her new EV? $20,000 — 5x × $4,000

This is the most-misunderstood part of valuation. Read it twice.

Why we use Enterprise Value, not just the stock price.

Equity Value (Market Cap) is what shareholders own. Enterprise Value is what the whole company costs to take over. When someone buys a business, they inherit its debt and pocket its cash — so the real price tag is bigger than the equity check.

Real-world EV may also add minority interest and preferred stock.
04 / 08 · WHY EV
Ratio 2 of 3 · What stock investors pay per dollar of earnings

Price / Earnings

Now switch sides. P/E is the most-quoted ratio in public markets. It tells you how many years of current earnings you're paying for. Where EV/EBITDA looks at the whole company, P/E looks only at the equity slice — the part shareholders actually own.

Watch out: P/E ignores debt. A company drowning in debt can show a "cheap" P/E that's actually a trap — because the equity is cheap precisely because the company owes so much. Always check the balance sheet before judging a P/E.
Citrus Co. is paying 6.2 years of current earnings for Maya's equity. The S&P 500 historical average sits around 17x — small private businesses typically trade lower because they're riskier and harder to sell.

05 / 08 · P / E
Ratio 3 of 3 · The fallback when earnings get messy

EV / Sales · the fallback multiple

EV/EBITDA and P/E both depend on the company actually making money. What about businesses that aren't profitable yet? Or ones whose earnings swing wildly year to year? That's where EV/Sales comes in — it's the multiple analysts reach for when the bottom line is too unreliable to use.

When to use it Rule of thumb
Reach for EV/Sales when EBITDA is negative or volatile — early-stage businesses, turnarounds, cyclical lows. Revenue is the most stable line on the income statement, so it stays usable when everything below it gets noisy. Common in software, biotech, and pre-profit startups.

06 / 08 · EV / SALES
Before you trust any multiple

How to judge whether a valuation multiple is "fair"

01
Find true comparables
A peer is a company with similar growth, margins, and risk — apples to apples. A lemonade stand doesn't compare to Coca-Cola, but it does compare to a small juice operation. Wrong peers, wrong multiple.
02
Check the historical range
Has this multiple traded between 3x and 8x over the last 5 years? Where in that range is it now? A 5x multiple is cheap in some industries, expensive in others — history sets the bar.
03
Ask why the multiple is what it is
Higher growth → higher multiple. More risk → lower multiple. Bigger moat → higher multiple. The number isn't random — there's a reason behind every premium and every discount.
Apply this framework to every valuation multiple you compute. The number alone tells you nothing. The number + peers + history + driver tells you everything.
