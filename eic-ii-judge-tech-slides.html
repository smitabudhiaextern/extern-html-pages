Judge the technology

Take Phaidra. The core technology is simple to state: reinforcement-learning software that sits on top of a data center's existing cooling controls and continuously tunes them. The current alternative is to keep the hard-coded controls a technician tunes by hand, which drift and don't adapt as the load and weather change. Phaidra's pitch is a 25 to 30% cut in cooling energy, versus the 2 to 5% a technician gets tuning by hand. Read that 25 to 30% as Phaidra's own claim. If it holds up, it is not a marginal gain, it is the size of improvement an operator acts on.

But notice the word if. That 25 to 30% figure is the company's own claim, and no independent field data backs it publicly. So you would write it down as a claim to verify, not a settled fact. The same goes for the pedigree: Phaidra traces its technology to Google DeepMind's cooling project, which the company says cut Google's cooling energy by 40%, but that lineage and that number are the company's claim too, with no independent field data public. Labeling a company's own claims as claims, until you can check them, is the single habit that separates a careful analyst from a cheerleader.

One more move marks the careful analyst: name where the technology does not work. Every technology has limits. Phaidra suits liquid-cooled AI data centers and large multi-site operators that already have the telemetry the model needs, and it's a weak fit for small edge sites with no instrumentation. Saying so isn't being negative. It's what an investor expects, and it's how you show you actually understand the technology instead of just repeating the pitch.
