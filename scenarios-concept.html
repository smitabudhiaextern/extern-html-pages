Section 1 of 4
Why one forecast isn't enough

Why one forecast isn't enough.

A single intrinsic value can't tell a portfolio manager what to do with it.

When you built the 5-year forecast for Alight, you projected revenue, costs, and profit out to 2030. Those numbers fed into a DCF model, which gave you one intrinsic value: $9.50 per share. Clean answer to "what is Alight worth?"

The problem is that single number doesn't tell anyone what to do with it. Walk up to a portfolio manager and say "Alight is worth $9.50." Their first question: "OK, but what if you're wrong? And if you're right, how right could you be?"

The PM isn't being annoying. They have to decide how much money to put into the stock. A stock that might be worth between $9 and $10 is a very different bet from one that might be worth between $5 and $14. The first is a small, controlled position. The second is a real swing.

A single forecast can't answer those questions. Scenarios can — that's what they exist to do. A scenario is a structured way of saying "here's what happens if things go well, here's what happens if they go badly, here's the most likely path." Three numbers instead of one.

Key insight

Investors don't pay for a guess. They pay for a guess with a range. Scenarios are how you turn a single $9.50 into a defensible $5–$14 range that a PM can actually trade on.

Section 2 of 4 · Part A
Two projections, two different jobs

First, what the 5-year forecast actually does.

It feeds the DCF model, which produces one intrinsic value. Long horizon. Single story.

The 5-year forecast lives in columns BL through BO. It's a year-by-year prediction for 2026, 2027, 2028, 2029, and 2030.

Its job is to feed the DCF model. A DCF works by taking many years of future cash flows and discounting them back to today's value. That math requires a long runway — you can't run a DCF on a single year, because most of a company's value comes from cash flows several years out. So you need 5 years of projections building toward a terminal value.

The output of the 5-year forecast → DCF chain is one intrinsic value number. For Alight, that's $9.50. The DCF says: "based on all the cash this company will generate from 2026 through 2030 and beyond, here's what it's worth today."

Long horizon. Single story. One number out. That's the job the 5-year forecast does. It tells you what the company is fundamentally worth.

Section 2 of 4 · Part B
Two projections, two different jobs

Now, what Bull/Base/Bear scenarios do.

They feed the price target. Short horizon. Three stories. Three numbers out.

Bull/Base/Bear scenarios live in columns BQ, BR, and BS. They all show FY2027 — same year, three sets of numbers.

Their job is to feed the price target. A price target answers a much narrower question than the DCF: "where could this stock trade in the next 12 to 18 months?" That's a short time horizon, so what matters is how the company performs in the very next year — because that's what the market will react to in the next two or three earnings cycles.

That's why Bull/Base/Bear only touches FY2027. There's no Bull/Base/Bear for 2028, 2029, or 2030 — those years are too far out to matter for a near-term trade.

Short horizon. Three stories. Three numbers out. That's the job scenarios do. They tell you what the stock could trade for, not what the company is worth.

Section 3 of 4
What changes between scenarios

What actually changes between Bull, Base, and Bear.

Most numbers look similar — that's the point. Only 5 cells flex; about 4 things stay locked.

Open columns BQ, BR, BS and the first thing you'll notice is that most numbers look very similar. Tax rate is the same. Interest rate is the same. Stock-based comp is the same. That's not laziness — that's the point.

A well-built scenario only flexes inputs that the underlying story changes. If your Bull thesis is "Alight wins new clients because their AI offering is differentiated," then revenue growth and margins should improve. But the tax rate? It doesn't care about Alight's product strategy. The interest rate on existing debt? Doesn't change because they won a contract. So those stay locked across all three columns.

In Alight's model, only 5 inputs flex across scenarios — see right. Each one is tied to either the company's operating story or to how the stock itself trades. Everything else stays constant.

Key insight

If you flex every input you can find, your scenarios become unauditable. Stick to the 4-5 cells that genuinely follow from your Bull/Bear narratives. A reviewer should be able to read your one-sentence thesis and see exactly which cells you changed because of it.

Section 4 of 4 · Part A
From inputs to three EPS numbers

From five inputs to three EPS numbers.

Change the inputs. The income statement re-runs. Three EPS values fall out.

Once you've changed those 5 inputs in each scenario column, the rest of Alight's income statement re-runs automatically. Revenue recalculates. Gross profit recalculates. Operating income recalculates. Net income recalculates. And finally, dividing net income by share count gives you the most important output of the whole exercise — EPS (earnings per share).

For Alight's FY2027 scenarios, the EPS values come out to $0.14 in Bull, $0.10 in Base, and −$0.21 in Bear (see right).

Notice that the Bear EPS is negative. That's not a mistake. It's what happens when revenue falls 4% while gross margin compresses from 37% to 30%. Operating income flips negative, which drags net income — and therefore EPS — below zero.

This is exactly the kind of downside investors want to know about. A Bear case that doesn't surface real pain isn't really a Bear case.

Section 4 of 4 · Part B
How EPS becomes a price target

How three EPS numbers become three price targets.

EPS isn't the final answer. Multiply by the P/E multiple, and you get a price.

EPS is the input into the price target. The formula is straightforward:

Price target = EPS × P/E multiple

The P/E multiple comes from comparable companies — what investors typically pay for $1 of earnings at a business like Alight. Suppose peers trade at roughly 100× adjusted EPS (illustrative number — you'd derive the real multiple from your comps work).

That gives you the three price targets shown on the right. Quick note on the Bear case: P/E doesn't work on negative earnings, so when EPS is negative you switch to a different valuation method (typically EV/EBITDA or book value). Math is different, principle is the same.

From today's $7 stock price, the PM now sees: $1 of downside, ~$3 of upside. That's a 3-to-1 risk-reward setup — positive enough to allocate to. If it were inverted (more downside than upside), they'd pass.

Key insight

Scenarios don't tell you what the stock will do. They tell you what it could do — and what the right risk-reward setup looks like. That's the difference between a guess and an investable trade.
